The fit answer is already in your reviews. Nobody has read all of them.

Every Huckberry jacket review quietly carries a structured fit rating and the shopper's own height, weight, and build. Pulled together, they say exactly how each jacket runs, and for whom. This is a read of that signal across three brands, built only from public review data.

Finding 01 · The flagship

The waxed trucker runs small, but not evenly

Across 1,377 reviewers who rated fit on the Flint & Tinder Flannel-Lined Waxed Trucker Jacket, 23.3% say it runs small and only 1.9% say it runs large. On its own that is a clean "size up" story. Broken out by build, it stops being one story.

Heavy-set reviewers report the jacket running small 41% of the time. Slim reviewers, 14%. A single "runs small" flag on the product page is right for one of these shoppers and wrong for the other. The fix is guidance that knows who is asking.

Finding 02 · Across the rack

Three brands, three different kinds of "runs small"

Huckberry sells brands it does not cut. Only the flagship collects a structured fit rating, so for Relwen and Barbour the fit read is pulled from review text by the model described below. Shown: of reviewers who comment on fit, the share who say the jacket runs small.

bar: % of fit mentions saying "runs small" 95% interval

All three lean small, but they are not the same problem. Flint & Tinder is nearly one-directional: almost no one calls it large. Barbour is contested, with real clusters of both "slim and tight" and "boxy, sleeves too long," the signature of a cut that fits some bodies and fights others. Relwen sits between, on a thin sample.

Relwen rests on 10 directional mentions and Barbour on 23, so their intervals are wide; read them as direction, not decimals. Flint & Tinder's text estimate (92% small) lands on top of its structured truth (93%), which is the point of the next section.

The check · Pressure-testing the AI

Why trust the text read at all

The cross-brand numbers lean on a model reading free text. Before trusting it, I tested it where truth exists: the flagship has both the review text and a structured fit rating. I had the model read only the text, guess the fit, and scored it against the rating it never saw.

Two things follow. Where a review talks about fit, the model is reliable, and its brand-level read of the flagship matches the ground truth almost exactly, which is what licenses using it on Relwen and Barbour. But half of reviews never mention fit even when the shopper clearly had an opinion they recorded in the structured field. Text alone misses that half. That gap is the argument for capturing fit as a field, or asking for it, rather than hoping people write it down.

What this is and isn't

Honest edges

  • Reviews are written by people who kept the jacket. Returners, the ones who matter most for fit, are mostly absent. This reads the fit of survivors.
  • The brand comparison is anchored by one richly-measured flagship and two thin text samples. It is a direction to check, not a verdict to ship.
  • The real outcome is returns, which live in your systems, not mine. Point this at return and exchange data and the "runs small" signal becomes a dollar figure instead of an inference.
  • Weight and height are self-reported and lightly cleaned. "The Rock" is not an athletic build I kept.

Fit is one field. The same approach reads durability complaints, color-accuracy gripes, and the words that precede a one-star, each sitting unused in the same review feed.
